Hi, my name is X. I'm a SULI intern working with the Environmental Sciences Division under my mentor, Andy Zhao, and today I'm going to talk to you about plastic.

Plastic is destroying the environment, but it's too useful for manufacturers to just stop producing outright. If we're ever going to stand a chance at lessening our depedence on it, we need some kind of alternative that ideally comes with little-to-no downsides.

One such alternative that researchers have pointed to time and time again is biocomposites. By the end of this talk, you'll learn the work my group has been doing to help biocomposites become a more widespread solution.

Where typical plastic composites are made using synthetic -- ususally petrolium based -- fibers, biocomposites use plant fibers. This comes with a good number of advantages. First of which: they're biodegradable, which makes them far more kind on the environment. Furthermore, with plants being everywhere, biocomposites are relatively cheap to produce, which gives them a wide appeal.

Unfortunately, they come with a major downside. Plants can be fairly variable -- no two members of a plant species are identical, and determining how a plant's unique characteristics affect the mechanical properties of the resulting composite requires expensive and difficult experimenting. So long as this is the case, biocomposites are never going to have the same prominence as synthetic composites.

What we need is some way of predicting the properties of biocomposites without needing so many difficult experiments. This is where machine learning presents itself as an interesting proposition. If we could train some kind of machine learning model to predict some of the mechanical properties of a composite given information about the input fiber, we'd be golden. There have been attempts to do this over the past couple of years, but they've largely all failed, blaming a lack of sufficient data to train the model on.

This is in spite of the large number of experiments that've been conducted and documented over the past 30-odd years. Indeed, although countless experiments have been performed, few if any are perfect in how much data they give us, or how they report said data. This makes curating datasets difficult, as valuable information our model needs in order to learn is often either not reported, or not reported in a way that's useful to us.

This puts us in a frustrating situation. We have this alternative to synthetic plastics that WOULD be nicer if only we knew how to predict the mechanical properties of the composite without making and testing it. We have a potential means of doing just that, but without the data for the model to train from, it'll never learn the relationship between the fiber's characteristics and the composite's mechanical properties.

Fixing the data problem isn't going to be quick or easy, but we need to start with something. Given just how close all of this is to coming together, it seems prudent to take a step back and analyze the state of the data we have. That's where my project comes in. Presently, we're performing an audit of widely available data to determine what variables are frequently reported, what variables are commonly missing, and how those holes affect the number of records we can use for training models.

Since most if not all of the data on past experiments is buried within research papers, our focus is extracting data from there. Presently, the process centers around a two-pass technique, where pass 1 involves a large-language model that parses the paper and flags any variables about the fiber or composite that the paper reports. This then aids pass 2, which is a manual pass that verifies the LLM's answers and makes note of what the paper contains, how it reports its data (tables, graphs, loose variables in the text, etc.), and any other notes about the paper itself that might be worth considering later. Once a large enough sampling of papers has been collected, we plan on analyzing the data we've collected to see what data we have and hypothesize why past attempts to train models might've struggled.

Ultimately, we hope that this will motivate further research aimed at performing experiments in the name of building large, more complete datasets. This would allow us to train models, accelerating biocomposite research and hopefully turning it into a far more applicable alternative to synthetic plastics. Thank you.
