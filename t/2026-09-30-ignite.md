I think we can all agree plastics suck, and they're kind of destroying the world right now. Unfortunately, they're also really useful, and if we're going to lessen our dependence on them we need an alternative that comes with, ideally, zero compromise. Biocomposites -- plastics made from plant fibers instead of synthetic fibers -- are an alternative that a lot of people are pointing to as the future, and it's easy to see why. Plants are everywhere and cheap to make things out of, plus they're biodegradable, so they're a lot more environmentally friendly. They do come with a major drawback, however, and that's how variable plants can be. No two members of a plant species are identical, and determining how a plant's unique properties affect the composite made from them can presently only be determined with difficult and expensive experiments.

So long as categorizing these composites is a chore, they'll never be adopted to anywhere near the scale that plastic has been. What would help is if we had a way of predicting how well a composite would perform given the plant fiber and polyer matrix we're using to make it without having to do the expensive experiments. In the short term, we could use this as a means of finding new potential composites to test, and in the long term it could work to lessen the need to perform these difficult experiments in the first place.

This is where machine learning presents itself as an interesting proposition. If we could train some kind of machine learning model to predict some qualities of a composite given information about the input fiber and polymer matrix, we'd be golden. There have been attempts to do this over the past couple of years, but almost all of them have failed, blaming a lack of sufficient data to train the model on -- without a sufficient amount of data, models can't figure out the relationship between the input and the output, and so their attempts result in them just memorizing the data they're being given, which makes them useless when they're given data they're not familiar with. There's also the surprisingly unspoken issue of encoding your input -- that is, turning the data into something the model understands. A lot of factors make a difference to the final composite, and not all of them are pretty numbers that models inherently understand, which is an additional challenge you need to overcome to successfully train models.

So we've got a bunch of researchers saying "we tried, but there's not enough data." But it's not like there's a shortage in research on this field; there's a good plethora of papers dating all the way back to 1993, and that's just what I've been able to survey so far in the past four or so weeks! If there's all of these papers detailing all of these experiments, testing all of these variables, how could there not be enough data to train models?

Well, for one thing, some data just... doesn't get reported. This matters because we don't know for sure what properties of the fiber or the polymer influence certain qualities of the composite the most -- like, what makes for a strong composite? We can certainly guess, and we know some things make a difference, but we don't know every single factor. In a lot of cases variables that may well have an impact, like say the diameter or the cellulose content of the fibers, often don't get reported in these experiments. So if there is some kind of connection between these variables and the properties of the resulting composite, our model won't be able to determine it from the data it has.

Of course, even when data is reported, though, it's not always perfect. Some papers aren't clear about how they create or process their composites, some contain conflicting information -- y'know, one figure given in the abstract, but then the tables say something slightly different, and some appear to just flat out have mistakes in them -- my favorite was a paper that, for reasons I've since forgotten, claimed that 20 GPa of pressure was exerted onto the composite during its creation, which is roughly 200,000 times atmospheric pressure at sea level. Or way more than most any material should be able to withstand... We assume that was a typo for MPa, but that paper was published 20 years ago and they never put out a correction, so who's to say. There are also some aspects of biocomposites that are underrepresented. If, for example, you're performing experiments and your testing concludes that some treatment or mixture produces a terrible composite, you're probably not going to report that.  Consequently, there's not a lot of data available to what makes composites worse.

So we're left at this crossroad, where we have SOME data, but it's not enough data, or it's not enough quality data, or it's a lot but only in some places and not others. Therefore, it seems like what we need to do now is look over what we have and give it a proper analysis. What data gets reported the most? What data isn't being reported? How does having some things and not having other things affect our ability to train our  dream model? The hope is that by sitting down and putting into writing what we have and what we need it'll kickstart work into doing research and experiments for the sake of building up a more thorough collection of data.
